
\section{Lower Bound}
\label{sec:lower}

Throughout the paper, we have build our polynomial evaluation programs as straight-line programs,
like
Motzkin's degree 4 example:
\begin{align}
   u_1 &= x (x + a_0), \\
   u_2 &= (u_1 + x + a_1)(x + a_2), \\
   P &= u_2 + a_3,
\end{align}
which showed that degree 4 can be done with 2 multiplications.

It is known that degree $2n+1$ polynomials require at least $n$ multiplications~\cite{paterson1973evaluation}.
We conjecture that with rational decoding, \emph{degree $2n$ polynomials require at least $n+1$ mu
ltiplications},
which would show that the small overhead in our algorithm is necessary.

In this section we prove the conjecture for degree~6 (i.e., that 3 multiplications do not suffice)
.
The general case remains open; we outline a potential approach in \cref{sec:lower:general}.

\subsection{Degree 6 with 3 Multiplications}


Consider a polynomial chain with three multiplications:
\begin{align}
   y&=x(y^{(2)}_x x + a_0)
   \\
   z&=(z^{(1)}_y y + z^{(1)}_x x + a_1)
      (z^{(2)}_y y + z^{(2)}_x x + a_2)
      \\
   w&=(w^{(1)}_z z + w^{(1)}_y y + w^{(1)}_x x + a_3)
      (w^{(2)}_z z + w^{(2)}_y y + w^{(2)}_x x + a_4)
   \\
   P &= P_w w + P_z z + P_y y + P_x x + a_5.
\end{align}
note we don't count the multiplications by the parameters $y_x$, $z_y$, etc.

Set $a_5=0$ and, by scaling the leading factors, assume $w^{(1)}_z=1$ and $P_w=1$ (absorbing const
ants into $w$).
Introduce the shorthand
\[
   A:=z+w^{(1)}_y y+w^{(1)}_x x+a_3,\quad
   B:=w^{(2)}_z z+w^{(2)}_y y+w^{(2)}_x x+a_4,\quad
   F:=y+z^{(1)}_x x+a_1,\quad
   G:=z^{(2)}_y y+z^{(2)}_x x+a_2.
\]
Then the parameter–derivatives that will form the rows of our matrix are
\[
   \frac{\partial P}{\partial a_4}=A,\qquad
   \frac{\partial P}{\partial a_3}=B,\qquad
   \frac{\partial P}{\partial a_2}=\bar z\,F,\qquad
   \frac{\partial P}{\partial a_1}=\bar z\,G,\qquad
   \frac{\partial P}{\partial a_0}=\bar y\,x,\qquad
   \frac{\partial P}{\partial a_5}=1,
\]
where
\[
   \bar z:=\frac{\partial P}{\partial z}=P_z+B+w^{(2)}_zA,\qquad
   \bar y:=\frac{\partial P}{\partial y}=P_y+w^{(1)}_y B+w^{(2)}_y A+\bar z\,(G+z^{(2)}_y F).
\]

Our goal is to show that for suitable $b,c$ with $b+c=-1$ and a suitable choice of parameters $a_0
,\dots,a_4$ we have
\[
   b\,\nabla P(-1)+\nabla P(0)+c\,\nabla P(1)=0.
\]
This implies that the three columns $\nabla P(-1)$, $\nabla P(0)$ and $\nabla P(1)$ are linearly d
ependent and hence the $3\times 6$ Jacobian block has zero determinant.

Another observation (from the original draft) is that any program containing a factor $v=(P+a_i)(P
+a_j)$ will not work; in particular we must have
\[
   D_z := \begin{vmatrix} 1 & z^{(1)}_x \\ z^{(2)}_y & z^{(2)}_x \end{vmatrix} \neq 0.
\]

Note that $\frac{\partial P}{\partial a_5}=1$, so the condition $b+c=-1$ is necessary. We will alw
ays set $a_5=0$.

We proceed by cases. Throughout we use the abbreviations
\[
   D_w :=
   \begin{vmatrix}
      1 & w^{(1)}_y \\
      w^{(2)}_z & w^{(2)}_y
   \end{vmatrix}
   ,\qquad
   D_z :=
   \begin{vmatrix}
      1 & z^{(1)}_x \\
      z^{(2)}_y & z^{(2)}_x
   \end{vmatrix}
   .
\]

\paragraph{Case 1: $w^{(2)}_z\neq 0$ and $D_w\neq 0$.}
Take $a_0=0$ and choose
\begin{align}
   b&= -\frac{\,w^{(2)}_x+w^{(2)}_y-(w^{(1)}_x+w^{(1)}_y)w^{(2)}_z\,}{2D_w},\\
   c&= -\frac{\,w^{(2)}_x-w^{(2)}_y-(w^{(1)}_x-w^{(1)}_y)w^{(2)}_z\,}{2D_w}.
\end{align}
Then one can pick $a_1,a_2,a_3,a_4$ as rational functions of the program parameters (with denomina
tors involving $w^{(2)}_z$, $D_w$ and $D_z$) so that $b\,\nabla P(-1)+\nabla P(0)+c\,\nabla P(1)=0
$.

\paragraph{Case 2: $D_w=0$ and $w^{(2)}_z\neq 0$.}
Here $w^{(2)}_y=w^{(2)}_z w^{(1)}_y$; take $b=c=-\tfrac12$ and $a_0=0$.
Setting either $a_3=0$ or $a_4=0$ and solving for the remaining $a$'s yields the desired identity.

\paragraph{Case 3: $w^{(2)}_z=0$ (necessarily $w^{(2)}_y\neq 0$).}
Take
\[
   b=-1,\qquad c=0,\qquad a_4=-P_z,\qquad a_3=\frac{P_z w^{(1)}_y-P_y}{\,w^{(2)}_y\,}.
\]
With $w^{(1)}_z=1$ and $a_4=-P_z$ we have $\bar z=P_z+B=P_z+(w^{(2)}_y y+w^{(2)}_x x - P_z)=w^{(2)
}_y y+w^{(2)}_x x$.
Thus the condition $-\nabla P(-1)+\nabla P(0)=0$ reduces to the following identities at $x\in\{-1,
0\}$:
\begin{align}
   &\text{(i)}\; B(-1)=B(0),\qquad
   \text{(ii)}\; A(-1)=A(0),\\
   &\text{(iii)}\; \bar z(-1)F(-1)=\bar z(0)F(0),\;\; \bar z(-1)G(-1)=\bar z(0)G(0),\\
   &\text{(iv)}\; \bar y(-1)=0.
\end{align}
Because $x(0)=0$, (iv) is exactly what is needed for the $a_0$-row; our choice of $a_3$ makes
\[
   P_y+w^{(1)}_y B+w^{(2)}_y A = 0\quad\text{at}\ x\in\{-1,0\},
\]
and since $\bar z(-1)=\bar z(0)$ by (i), the $\bar z(\cdot)(\,\cdot\,)$-part in $\bar y$ does not
affect (iv).

We now split Case 3 in two subcases.

\emph{Case 3a (aligned slope): $w^{(2)}_x = w^{(2)}_y z^{(1)}_x$.}
Set
\[
   a_0=1-z^{(1)}_x,\qquad a_2=0,\qquad
   a_1=\frac{\,w^{(1)}_x - w^{(1)}_y z^{(1)}_x\,}{\,D_z\,},\quad D_z=z^{(2)}_x-z^{(2)}_y z^{(1)}_x
\neq 0.
\]
With this $a_0$ and the relation $w^{(2)}_x = w^{(2)}_y z^{(1)}_x$ one checks directly that (i) ho
lds.
With $a_2=0$ we have $z(0)=0$, and the choice of $a_1$ is exactly the unique solution of
\(
  z(-1) + w^{(1)}_y y(-1) + w^{(1)}_x(-1)=0,
\)
i.e. (ii). Then $\bar z(-1)=\bar z(0)=0$ (since $B(-1)=B(0)=-P_z$), so (iii) is automatic. Finally
, with the chosen $a_3$ we get $\bar y(-1)=0$, so (iv) holds. Hence $-\nabla P(-1)+\nabla P(0)=0$.

\emph{Case 3b (generic slope): $w^{(2)}_x \neq w^{(2)}_y z^{(1)}_x$.}
Take
\[
   a_1=0,\qquad a_0=\frac{w^{(2)}_y-w^{(2)}_x}{\,w^{(2)}_y\,}=1-\frac{w^{(2)}_x}{w^{(2)}_y}.
\]
This makes (i) hold, and hence $\bar z(-1)=\bar z(0)=0$, so (iii) is automatic.
To enforce (ii), solve the single linear equation
\[
   A(-1)=A(0)\;\Longleftrightarrow\; z(-1)+w^{(1)}_y y(-1)+w^{(1)}_x(-1)=0
\]
for $a_2$. Evaluating with $a_1=0$ and $a_0=1-\tfrac{w^{(2)}_x}{w^{(2)}_y}$ yields the explicit va
lue
\[
   \boxed{\;
   a_2
   =
   \frac{\big(z^{(2)}_x w^{(2)}_y - z^{(2)}_y w^{(2)}_x\big)\big(w^{(2)}_x - w^{(2)}_y z^{(1)}_x\big)
         - w^{(2)}_y\big(w^{(1)}_y w^{(2)}_x - w^{(1)}_x w^{(2)}_y\big)}
        {\,w^{(2)}_y\big(w^{(2)}_x - w^{(2)}_y z^{(1)}_x\big)\,}\;}
\]
(note the denominator $w^{(2)}_y\big(w^{(2)}_x - w^{(2)}_y z^{(1)}_x\big)$).
With this $a_2$ we have (ii), and together with the earlier choices of $a_3,a_4$ this implies (iv)
 as above. Hence $-\nabla P(-1)+\nabla P(0)=0$.

\medskip
In every case we have found $b,c$ with $b+c=-1$ and parameters $a_0,\dots,a_4$ (depending rational
ly on the program coefficients) such that
\[
   \big[\,\nabla P(-1)\;\; \nabla P(0)\;\; \nabla P(1)\,\big]
   \begin{bmatrix} b\\[2pt]1\\[2pt]c\end{bmatrix}
   =0.
\]
Therefore the three columns are linearly dependent and the determinant is zero. This shows that th
ree multiplications do not suffice for degree~6.

\subsection{Towards a General Lower Bound (Work in Progress)}
\label{sec:lower:general}

This section outlines a potential approach to proving the general conjecture.
The argument is incomplete; we include it to document the proof strategy and invite further work.

We consider any straight-line program computing a polynomial
given $x, a_0, a_1, \dots, a_{2n-1} \in \mathbb{F}$ as input.

\begin{align}
   u_0 &= x \\
   u_1 &=
      (\theta_{1,0} u_0 + \sum_i \gamma_{1,i} a_i)
      (\theta'_{1,0} u_0 + \sum_i \gamma'_{1,i} a_i)
   \\
   u_2 &=
      (\theta_{2,1} u_1 + \theta_{2,0} u_0 + \sum_i \gamma_{2,i} a_i)
      (\theta'_{2,1} u_1 + \theta'_{2,0} u_0 + \sum_i \gamma'_{2,i} a_i)
   \\
   \dots
   \\
   P &= \sum_{i=0}^{n} \theta_{n,i} u_j + \sum_i \gamma_{n,i} a_i.
\end{align}

Here the $\theta$ and $\gamma$ coefficients are arbitrary constants in $\mathbb{F}$,
which captures that we can do unlimited additions and subtractions for free.
In a sense this is stronger than what we need, but it doesn't cause any issues in the proof.


We get that $P_{\theta,\gamma}(x,a)$ is a polynomial in $x$ and $a_0, a_1, \dots, a_{2n-1}$.
The goal is to show that for some two $x_1 \neq x_2$ there exist $a$ values such that
\[
   P_{\theta,\gamma}(x_1, a) = P_{\theta,\gamma}(x_2, a).
\]
We will show this, which implies the following theorem:

\begin{theorem}
\label{thm:lower-bound}
   Assume $d$ is even and $d\ge 6$.
   If for all sets of $d$ distinct values $x_0,\dots,x_{d-1}$
   the map $F_x(a) = a \mapsto (P_a(x_0),\dots,P_a(x_{d-1}))$ is a polynomial automorphism,
   then $m \ge d/2+1$.
\end{theorem}

Let
\[
   J = \begin{matrix}
      \frac{dP(x_1)}{da_0}
      &\frac{dP(x_2)}{da_0}
      &\dots
      \\
      \frac{dP(x_1)}{da_1}
      &\frac{dP(x_2)}{da_1}
      &\dots
      \\
      \vdots
      &\vdots
      &\ddots
   \end{matrix}
\]
by the Jacobian matrix of $P(x)$.
The theorem follows if we can show that for some choice of $x_1,\dots,x_{2n}$ and $a_0,\dots,a_{2n
-1}$ that for all $\gamma$ and $\theta$ we have
a zero determinant:
\[
   |J|=0.
\]
\begin{proof}
   Since P is an automorphism, we have
   \(
      P^{-1} \circ P = \mathrm{id}.
   \)
   Differentiate both sides at a point (x):
   \[
      D(P^{-1})(P(x)) \cdot DP(x) = I.
   \]
   Taking determinants:
   \[
      \det D(P^{-1})(P(x)) \cdot \det DP(x) = 1.
   \]
   This immediately implies:
   \(
      \det DP(x) \neq 0
   \)
   for all $x$.
\end{proof}

In the case of Motzkin's construction, we get
$P(x,a) = x^4 + a_2 x^3 + (a_1 - a_0) x^2 + (a_2 - a_0 a_1) x + a_3$.
It's easy to see that if we want $P(x) = x^4 + c_3 x^3 + c_2 x^2 + c_1 x + c_0$, we can
take
\begin{align}
   a_3 &= c_0,
   \\
   a_0 &= c_3.
   \\
   a_1 &= c_2 + a_0 = c_2 + c_3,
   \\
   a_2 &= c_1 + a_0 a_1 = c_1 + c_3(c_2 + c_3).
\end{align}
We can thus define the polynomial ``decoder map'':
\[
   a = \Phi(c) = (c_3, c_2 + c_3, c_1 + c_3(c_2 + c_3), c_0).
\]

Whenever such a decoder map exists, we can assume we first diagonalize the $\gamma$ and $\gamma'$
matrices.
We can also assume that the first step is never $u_1 = (x+a_0) (x + a_1)$, since this would be sym
metric in $a_0$ and $a_1$ and we would never be able to distinguish them.
That allows us to assume the simpler form
\begin{align}
   u_0 &= x \\
   u_1 &= x (x + a_0)
   \\
   u_2 &=
      (\theta_{2,1} u_1 + \theta_{2,0} u_0 + a_1)
      (\theta'_{2,1} u_1 + \theta'_{2,0} u_0 + a_2)
   \\
   \dots
   \\
   P &= \sum_{i=0}^{n} \theta_{n,i} u_j + a_{2n-1}.
\end{align}
To simplify further, we rewrite this as
\begin{align}
   u_0 &= x \\
   u_1 &= x(R_{1,0} u_{0} + a_{1}) \\
   \ell_i &= L_i u_{<i} + a_{i},
   \quad
   r_i = R_i u_{<i}+b_{i} \\
   u_i &= \ell_i\,r_i
    \quad (\text{for } i=2,\dots,m) \\
   P &= C u_{\le m} + b_{1},
\end{align}

The goal is to find a vector $c\in \mathbb{F}^{2n}$ such that
\[
   J c = 0.
\]

As a first step, we write out the partial derivatives using the chain rule / back-propagation:
Let's write $\bar u_i = \frac{dP}{du_i}$.
Then by the chain rule we have
\begin{align}
   \bar{u_i} &= C_i + \sum_{j>i}
   \bar u_j (L_{j,i} r_j + R_{j,i} \ell_j)
   \\
   \frac{dP}{da_1} &= \bar u_1 x \\
   \frac{dP}{da_i} &= \bar u_i r_i, \quad
   \frac{dP}{db_i} = \bar u_i \ell_i \quad \text{for } i=2,\dots,m
   \\
   \frac{dP}{db_1} &= 1
\end{align}
In particular we see
\[
   \bar u_m = C_m,
   \quad\text{and}\quad
   \bar u_{m-1} = C_{m-1} + \bar C_m (L_{m,m-1} r_m + R_{m,m-1} \ell_m).
\]


If we write up the Jacobian we get the following structure:
\[
   J =
   \begin{bmatrix}
      \bar{u_1}(x_1)x_1 &
      \bar{u_1}(x_2)x_2 &
       \dots &
      \bar{u_1}(x_{2m})x_{2m} \\
      \bar{u_2}(x_1) r_2(x_1) &
      \bar{u_2}(x_2) r_2(x_2) &
      \dots &
      \bar{u_2}(x_{2m}) r_2(x_{2m}) \\
      \bar{u_2}(x_1) \ell_2(x_1) &
      \bar{u_2}(x_2) \ell_2(x_2) &
      \dots &
      \bar{u_2}(x_{2m}) \ell_2(x_{2m}) \\
      \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\
      \bar{u_m}(x_1) r_m(x_1) &
      \bar{u_m}(x_2) r_m(x_2) &
      \dots &
      \bar{u_m}(x_{2m}) r_m(x_{2m}) \\
      \bar{u_m}(x_1) \ell_m(x_1) &
      \bar{u_m}(x_2) \ell_m(x_2) &
      \dots &
      \bar{u_m}(x_{2m}) \ell_m(x_{2m}) \\
      1 & 1 & \dots & 1
   \end{bmatrix}
\]

The first condition is $\langle 1, c\rangle=0$.

Next we want to set
\begin{align}
0=
\sum_{j=1}^{2n} c_j \bar{u_m}(x_j) \ell_m(x_j)
&=
C_m \sum_{j=1}^{2n} c_j (\langle L_m , u_{<m}(x_j)\rangle + a_m)
\\&=
C_m (\sum_{j=1}^{2n} c_j \langle L_m , u_{<m}(x_j) \rangle + a_m \sum_{j=1}^{2n} c_j)
\\&=
C_m \sum_{j=1}^{2n} c_j \langle L_m, u_{<m}(x_j)\rangle
,
\end{align}
where we used $\sum_{j=1}^{2n} c_j = 0$
by the first condition.
I suppose the last sum is a bilinear form in $L_m$ and $c$.
Note we also get
\[
   0 = C_m \sum_{j=1}^{2n} c_j \langle R_m, u_{<m}(x_j)\rangle
\]
by the other $\bar u_m$ row.
Both of these form will have to be set to zero by choosing $c$ appropriately, as $a_m$ and $b_m$ d
isappeared.

Now we look at the $\bar u_{m-1}$ rows.
We have the conditions
\begin{align}
   0&=
   \sum_{j=1}^{2n} c_j \bar u_{m-1} (x_j) \ell_{m-1}(x_j)
   \\&=
   \sum_{j=1}^{2n} c_j
      \left(
         C_{m-1} + \bar u_m (x_j) (L_{m,m-1} r_m(x_j) + R_{m,m-1} \ell_m(x_j))
      \right)
      \ell_{m-1}(x_j)
   \\&=
   % expand r_m and \ell_
   \sum_{j=1}^{2n} c_j
      \left(
         C_{m-1} + C_m (L_{m,m-1} (R_m u_{<m}(x_j) + b_m) + R_{m,m-1} (L_m u_{<m}(x_j) + a_m))
      \right)
      \ell_{m-1}(x_j)
   \\&=
   % then collect b_m and a_m
   b_m \sum_{j=1}^{2n} c_j C_m L_{m,m-1} \ell_{m-1}(x_j)
   \\&\quad
   + a_m \sum_{j=1}^{2n} c_j C_m R_{m,m-1} \ell_{m-1}(x_j)
   \\&\quad
   + \sum_{j=1}^{2n} c_j
      \left(
         C_{m-1} + C_m (L_{m,m-1} R_m + R_{m,m-1} L_m) u_{<m}(x_j)
      \right)
      \ell_{m-1}(x_j)
   ,
\end{align}
We see that we get two equations $d_1 a_m + d_2 b_m + d_3 = 0$ and $d_4 a_m + d_5 b_m + d_6 = 0$.
It is important to notice that the $r$ and $\ell$ values depend only on $a_{<m}$ and $b_{<m}$, so
we can freely pick $a_m$ and $b_m$ without changing them.
We can solve these using just $a_m$ and $b_m$ (no requirements on $c$) unless the determinant is z
ero:
\[
   \begin{vmatrix}
      d_1 & d_2 \\
      d_4 & d_5
   \end{vmatrix}
   =
   C_m
   \begin{vmatrix}
       R_{m,m-1} \sum_{j=1}^{2n} c_j \ell_{m-1}(x_j) &
       L_{m,m-1} \sum_{j=1}^{2n} c_j \ell_{m-1}(x_j) \\
       R_{m,m-1} \sum_{j=1}^{2n} c_j r_{m-1}(x_j) &
       L_{m,m-1} \sum_{j=1}^{2n} c_j r_{m-1}(x_j)
   \end{vmatrix}
   = 0
   .
\]
However, it is always zero, so again we are out of luck.

Maybe it's useful to introduce the notation $\langle f\rangle = \sum_{j=1}^{2n} c_j f(x_j)$?
In that case the determinant is just
\[
   C_m
   \begin{vmatrix}
       R_{m,m-1} \langle \ell_{m-1} \rangle &
       L_{m,m-1} \langle \ell_{m-1} \rangle \\
       R_{m,m-1} \langle r_{m-1} \rangle &
       L_{m,m-1} \langle r_{m-1} \rangle
   \end{vmatrix}
   =
   0
\]

Maybe we should instead look at what happens in the last (first) row.
We have
\begin{align}
   0 &= \langle \bar u_1 x \rangle
   \\&=
   \langle C_1 x + \sum_{j>1} \bar u_j (L_{j,1} r_j + R_{j,1} \ell_j) x \rangle
   \\&=
   \langle C_1 x \rangle
   + \sum_{j=2}^{m} \langle \bar u_j (L_{j,1} r_j + R_{j,1} \ell_j) x \rangle
\end{align}


Can we do anything with the fact that we've already enforced

   \langle \bar u_i r_i \rangle = 0,\quad
   \langle \bar u_i \ell_i \rangle = 0,\quad \text{for } i=m,m-1,\dots,2?
\]
For example, this should imply that

   \langle \bar u_{i} \rangle
   = \langle C_{i} \rangle + \sum_{j>i} \langle \bar u_j (L_{j,i} r_j + R_{j,i} \ell_j) \rangle
   = 0
\]

But unfortunately that doesn't seem to simplify that last $\langle \bar u_1 x \rangle$ expression
